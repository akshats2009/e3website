Chinese Labor and the Transcontinental Railroad: The Uncredited Contributions and Lost Legacy

By Minghao Gu · October 4, 2026

Banner: Chinese railroad workers near the opening of Summit Tunnel, 1865–1867. Photograph by Alfred A. Hart. Library of Congress.

In the winter of 1866, high in the Sierra Nevada Mountains, dozens of men dangled from wicker baskets against the icy walls of granite. Freezing winds swirled around them, drowning out the voices of the foremen. Below them, a canyon dropped away for hundreds of feet. Above them, snow piled in drifts over thirty feet deep. The task was simple: chip away at solid rock to carve out a path for a railroad that would soon stretch across the entire continent. This was the work of Chinese immigrants, and without them, the Transcontinental Railroad – one of the most ambitious engineering projects in history – would never have been built.

Periled Workers

In the early 1860s, the Central Pacific Railroad Company (CPRC) faced a significant labor shortage in building the western leg of the transcontinental line. Charles Crocker, co-owner of the CPRC alongside Leland Stanford, took what seemed at the time to be a risk. Against the advice of his colleagues, he hired Chinese laborers. Recruited from the Pearl River Delta region of Guangdong Province, these laborers were drawn by comparatively higher wages and the chance to escape the aftermath of the Opium Wars, ethnic conflict, and extreme poverty at home. By the railroad's completion, an estimated 15,000 workers had labored on the line, and over 13,000 of them were Chinese (Gilder Lehrman Institute). In short, Chinese immigrants didn't simply supplement the workforce. They became its backbone.

Despite driving the railroad's success, Chinese laborers faced the harshest working conditions on the project. They worked from dawn to dusk, six days a week, often battling natural hazards like avalanches and snowdrifts. They were also tasked with the most dangerous job on the line: blasting obstructive rock out of the mountains. The primary explosive was black powder, which required workers to be lowered by rope or wicker basket to place and light the charges, exposing them to constant risk of accidents and chemicals. In an extremely perilous case, workers at the Summit Tunnel in the Sierra Nevada were tasked with handling nitroglycerine, one of the most unstable and explosive chemical compounds at the time.

Resistance and Erasure

On June 25, 1867, over 3,000 Chinese workers went on strike at the Sierra Nevada worksite (Stanford News). The cause was discrimination. Chinese laborers were paid $35 per month, compared to $42 per month for white laborers, and were required to cover their own food and housing (Library of Congress). In response, strike leaders demanded $40 per month, a shorter workday reduced from eleven hours to ten, and generally reduced hours for work inside the tunnels.

Crocker responded ruthlessly, shutting down the strike within eight days (Library of Congress). He halted supply deliveries to the mountain camps, brought in sheriffs and deputies to threaten the strikers, and warned workers they would be fined out of their pay. For many Chinese workers, the risk of continued resistance outweighed the potential reward, and the strike collapsed. Still, it was not entirely unsuccessful: although the original demands went unmet, Chinese workers' wages gradually rose by the time the railroad was completed.

A little less than two years later, on May 10, 1869, in Promontory Summit, Utah, Leland Stanford connected the eastern and western sections of the railroad with a golden spike, marking the completion of the six-year project. Yet when the moment was immortalized on camera in the famous "Golden Spike" photograph, Chinese workers were nowhere to be found in the frame. It is, unfortunately, a fitting end to a story America's textbooks have never told in full. The men who once dangled from wicker baskets over the Sierra Nevada cliffs in cutting cold and snowstorms deserve more than a footnote. They deserve their own chapter, because history owes them recognition as the hands that built America.
